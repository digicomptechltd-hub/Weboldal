Week 1

The Problem

1/16 The meeting that cost three months

Laura walked into the meeting to approve a pilot. She walked out with a compliance task list and a three-month delay. The pilot never changed. The meeting did. Laura is VP of Operations at a mid-sized logistics company. She'd been building an AI tool to help her dispatch team prioritise routes — nothing exotic, no sensitive data, no automated decisions. A human dispatcher reviewed every output before acting. She booked a 30-minute steering meeting to get sign-off and move to pilot. Ninety minutes later, she walked out with no sign-off and a new action list: legal review, DPIA scoping, "high-risk assessment," and a follow-up meeting in three weeks. Nobody in that room was wrong. Legal was doing its job. The risk team was being responsible. Leadership wanted certainty before approving. But nobody had asked the four questions that would have resolved everything in ten minutes: What is the AI actually doing? (Ranking routes for a human dispatcher to review.) Does it make decisions or support them? (Support — the human decides.) Who is affected and how? (Internal team, no external impact.) What happens if it's wrong? (A dispatcher re-routes — recoverable, low-stakes.) Without those answers, the meeting had no anchor. So it escalated. This is how the EU AI Act costs organisations months — not through the law itself, but through the absence of a routing step before the compliance conversation starts. The fix takes about twelve questions and produces a score. From the score, you route: Transparency, PreCheck, or HRAI. Proportional to the actual risk — not the imagined worst case. Not legal advice. Not audit. Decision support.

2/16 One email, three weeks, €12,000

One email to Legal cost this team three weeks. The AI wasn't even high-risk. Marcus is Head of Product at a fintech scale-up. His team was shipping fast. One sprint, they added an AI feature — a model that flagged unusual account activity for a human compliance officer to review. The human reviewed every flag. No automated action. No customer-facing decision. Before pushing to production, Marcus sent one line to Legal: "Heads up — we're shipping an AI feature next sprint. Worth flagging for AI Act purposes." Responsible. Reasonable. The kind of thing you're supposed to do. Three weeks later, the sprint was still on hold. Legal had escalated to external advisers. External advisers had scoped a full EU AI Act high-risk assessment. The assessment would take four to six weeks and cost €12,000. When Marcus finally got on a call to review the situation, the classification took about fifteen minutes. The model flagged anomalies. A human reviewed every flag. No automated decisions. No direct customer impact. No Annex III trigger. Score: 14/60. Route: Transparency — baseline disclosure obligations, manageable internally. The €12,000 assessment was never needed. The three-week hold wasn't either. The problem wasn't that Legal was wrong. It was that "high-risk" became the assumed starting label — not the output of a structured assessment. In EU AI Act terms, high-risk classification requires intended purpose, decision impact, affected persons, oversight model, and Annex III signal review. Without those inputs, classification is a guess. And organisations are very conservative when they're guessing.

3/16 Two traps: freeze vs. over-compliance

Two companies. Same AI Act problem. One froze. One over-complied. Both lost six months. Neither had to. In the same week last autumn, I spoke with two different organisations facing the same situation: an AI tool approaching production, an upcoming EU AI Act deadline, and no clear path forward. Company A froze. Their legal team said "we can't clear this until we know the risk classification." Their product team said "we can't classify it until Legal tells us what to look for." The project sat in that loop for six months. Company B over-complied. A well-meaning Head of Compliance launched a full governance programme — external advisers, documentation sprints, weekly steering committees. Three months in, the AI tool's scope had been reduced three times to avoid anticipated obligations. The programme cost more than the AI project itself. What both companies were missing was the same thing: a routing step that could answer, within a structured 12-question assessment, where the use case actually sat on the risk spectrum. Company A's AI tool scored 9/60 — Transparency route. No freeze needed. Company B's AI tool scored 31/60 — PreCheck route. Targeted clarification, not a full governance programme. Neither needed what they did. Both needed what they didn't have: a proportional starting point derived from actual signals, not from assumption. That's what the EU AI Act's risk-based approach is designed to produce. Proportionality isn't a loophole — it's the design principle. Both traps — freeze and over-compliance — have the same fix: a routing step that takes 3 minutes and costs a fraction of what either trap does.

4/16 "What do you need me to decide?"

She sat through a 45-minute compliance presentation. Then asked one question. The room went quiet. The CEO of a 220-person manufacturing company had asked her compliance manager to present the AI Act readiness status. He'd prepared well — forty-five minutes of regulatory context, risk categories, control frameworks, reference legislation. She listened. She asked one question at the end. "So — what do you need me to decide?" The room went quiet. Not because the question was unfair. Because the presentation hadn't been built to answer it. There was no decision on the table. No options with trade-offs. No recommended next step with an owner and a deadline. No "if we proceed, here's what needs to happen; if we don't, here's the cost." The presentation was correct. It just wasn't actionable. And at leadership level, correct and actionable are not the same thing. A decision-ready AI Act output answers three questions: (1) Decision — approve, pause, redesign, or escalate — with a clear recommendation. (2) Delegation — who owns what, with a deliverable and a deadline. (3) Documentation — what evidence is needed, when it's needed, and who creates it. When those three things are present, the CEO can act in ten minutes. Without them, she reschedules. The answer to "what do you need me to decide?" is the output of a structured routing assessment.

Week 3

The Method

9/16 What 12 questions actually measure

"You keep saying '12 questions' like that's meaningful. What are they actually measuring?" A fair challenge. Here's the honest answer. The 12 dimensions aren't measuring whether you have documents. They're measuring signal density — how many indicators of genuine compliance risk are present in your specific use case. Here's what each dimension covers: 1. Personal data processing — does the AI touch personal data? 2. Special category data — health, biometric, or financial data in input or output? 3. Automated decision-making — does the AI decide, or does a human decide informed by the AI? 4. Impact on user rights — can the output affect access to services, employment, education, or financial products? 5. Life-situation decisions — does the output affect someone's material circumstances directly? 6. Error consequences — what happens when the model is wrong? 7. Human oversight need — is there a defined human review point? 8. Data quality dependency — does reliability depend on variable input data quality? 9. Continuous monitoring need — does the risk profile change over time? 10. Behaviour influence — does the AI influence user decisions in ways that create accountability? 11. Logging and auditability — can decisions and outputs be reconstructed and reviewed? 12. Regulatory and compliance risk — are there sector-specific obligations that add to the baseline? Each dimension is scored. The combination produces a 0–60 total. The total — combined with critical flags — determines the routing: Transparency, PreCheck, or HRAI. It's not a checklist. It's a signal map.

10/16 Same AI tool, score 8 vs. score 41

Two companies. Almost identical AI tools. One scored 8/60. The other scored 41/60. Here's why — and what it meant for what each one had to do next. Both organisations had built an AI tool that did, on the surface, the same thing: it analysed incoming customer service requests and routed them to the appropriate team. Company A — score 8/60 → Transparency The AI only routed internal ticket categories, not customer-facing decisions. Human agents reviewed all routings before acting. No personal data was processed — only anonymised ticket content. No Annex III trigger. Route: Transparency. An AI disclosure notice and a basic logging process. Two weeks of work. Company B — score 41/60 → PreCheck The AI routed customer service requests that included account decisions — credit limit changes, service suspensions. Routing sometimes triggered automated actions without a human review step. Personal data was processed. Financial services sector flag activated. Route: PreCheck. Oversight documentation, Annex III clarification, GDPR processing note, and a routing decision on whether deeper HRAI assessment was needed. Same surface function. Fundamentally different risk profile. The score is not a judgment. It's a map. It shows where the risk signals are concentrated — and therefore where the compliance work needs to go.

Week 4

The Output

13/16 What to do tomorrow morning

Most compliance plans tell you where to end up. This one tells you what to do tomorrow morning. "I always know what needs to be done eventually. Annex III assessment, oversight documentation, DPIA review — I know the list. What I never know is what to do tomorrow morning." That gap — between knowing the destination and knowing the next step — is where most AI Act compliance programmes stall. The 7-day implementation plan in every AI Compliance Engine report is designed to close that gap. Day 1 — Use case definition. Document the intended purpose and actual purpose. Assign owners: Business, IT, Compliance. Day 2 — Scope and exclusions. Define the system boundary. First version of the change management approach. Day 3 — Automation and human control. Document whether the AI recommends, supports, or acts automatically. Map the human oversight and escalation points. Day 4 — Data processing. Map data sources, personal data, access rights. Review logging and retention logic. Day 5 — Annex III pre-screening. Review which categories apply, collect the "needs clarification" points. Day 6 — GDPR / DPIA trigger check. Review automated decision points, profiling, and special category data. Day 7 — Routing decision. Management decision: stay at PreCheck level, or open HRAI / DPIA / legal assessment? Document next steps and assign owners. By day 3, the conversation changes. By day 7, leadership has something they can approve, redirect, or escalate — not just read.

EU AI Act Context & Official References EU-KI-Verordnung: Kontext & offizielle Quellen EU AI Act kontextus & hivatalos hivatkozások

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 — the EU AI Act — establishes a tiered, risk-based regulatory framework for AI systems placed on or put into service in the EU market. It distinguishes between prohibited AI practices, high-risk AI systems, general purpose AI (GPAI) models, and lower-risk applications.

AI transparency obligations under Article 50 apply to certain AI systems regardless of risk classification, covering disclosure requirements in human-facing interactions.

Administrative fines may reach up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover, depending on the nature and severity of the violation. These thresholds are subject to supervisory authority discretion and proportionality assessment.

Official EU AI Act regulation (EUR-Lex) — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

Die Verordnung (EU) 2024/1689 — die EU-KI-Verordnung — schafft einen abgestuften, risikobasierten Rechtsrahmen für KI-Systeme, die auf dem EU-Markt in Verkehr gebracht oder in Betrieb genommen werden. Sie unterscheidet verbotene KI-Praktiken, Hochrisiko-KI-Systeme, KI-Modelle mit allgemeinem Verwendungszweck (GPAI) und Anwendungen mit geringerem Risiko.

Transparenzpflichten gemäß Artikel 50 gelten für bestimmte KI-Systeme unabhängig von der Risikoeinstufung und umfassen Offenlegungspflichten bei Interaktionen mit Personen.

Verwaltungsstrafen können bis zu 35 Millionen Euro oder 7 % des weltweiten Jahresumsatzes betragen. Diese Schwellenwerte unterliegen dem Ermessen der Aufsichtsbehörde und einer Verhältnismäßigkeitsprüfung.

Offizielle EU-KI-Verordnung (EUR-Lex) — Verordnung (EU) 2024/1689

Az (EU) 2024/1689 rendelet — az EU AI Act — többszintű, kockázatalapú szabályozási keretet hoz létre az EU piacon forgalomba hozott vagy üzembe helyezett AI rendszerek számára. Megkülönbözteti a tiltott AI gyakorlatokat, a magas kockázatú AI rendszereket, az általános célú AI (GPAI) modelleket és az alacsonyabb kockázatú alkalmazásokat.

Az 50. cikk szerinti AI átláthatósági kötelezettségek bizonyos AI rendszerekre kockázati besorolástól függetlenül érvényesek, lefedve a személyekkel folytatott interakciókra vonatkozó közzétételi követelményeket.

A közigazgatási bírságok elérheti a 35 millió eurót vagy a globális éves forgalom 7%-át, a jogsértés jellegétől és súlyosságától függően. E küszöbértékek a felügyeleti hatóság mérlegelési jogköre és arányossági értékelés hatálya alá esnek.

What is AI Compliance? Was ist KI-Compliance? Mi az AI megfelelés?

AI compliance refers to the structured alignment of AI system usage with applicable legal, regulatory and governance requirements, including the EU AI Act and GDPR. It focuses on documented, proportional and traceable decisions rather than certification.

KI-Compliance bezeichnet die strukturierte Ausrichtung des Einsatzes von KI-Systemen an rechtlichen, regulatorischen und Governance-Anforderungen, insbesondere der EU-KI-Verordnung und der DSGVO. Sie konzentriert sich auf dokumentierte, verhältnismäßige und nachvollziehbare Entscheidungen und nicht auf Zertifizierung.

Az AI megfelelés a mesterséges intelligencia rendszerek használatának strukturált igazítása a vonatkozó jogi, szabályozási és governance követelményekhez, beleértve az EU AI Act-et és a GDPR-t. A hangsúly a dokumentált, arányos és nyomon követhető döntéseken van, nem a tanúsításon.

What is AI Governance? Was ist KI-Governance? Mi az AI governance?

AI governance defines how AI systems are designed, deployed, monitored and reviewed, including roles, responsibilities and escalation paths.

KI-Governance definiert, wie KI-Systeme konzipiert, eingesetzt, überwacht und überprüft werden, einschließlich Rollen, Verantwortlichkeiten und Eskalationswegen.

Az AI governance az AI rendszerek tervezésének, üzembe helyezésének, felügyeletének és felülvizsgálatának módját határozza meg, beleértve a szerepköröket, felelősségeket és eszkalációs útvonalakat.

What is the EU AI Act (2024/1689)? Was ist die EU-KI-Verordnung (2024/1689)? Mi az EU AI Act (2024/1689)?

The EU AI Act establishes a risk-based regulatory framework for AI systems with progressive enforcement between 2025 and 2026.

Die EU-KI-Verordnung schafft einen risikobasierten Rechtsrahmen für KI-Systeme in der EU mit schrittweiser Durchsetzung zwischen 2025 und 2026.

Az EU AI Act kockázatalapú szabályozási keretet hoz létre az AI rendszerek számára, fokozatos végrehajtással 2025 és 2026 között.

What is Annex III of the EU AI Act? Was ist Anhang III der EU-KI-Verordnung? Mi az EU AI Act Annex III-a?

Annex III lists AI use cases that may qualify as High-Risk AI depending on purpose and context.

Anhang III listet KI-Anwendungsfälle auf, die je nach Zweck und Kontext als Hochrisiko-KI eingestuft werden können.

Az Annex III a potenciálisan magas kockázatú AI felhasználási területeket sorolja fel, amelyek minősítése a céltól és a kontextustól függ.

What is High-Risk AI (HRAI)? Was ist Hochrisiko-KI (HRAI)? Mi a magas kockázatú AI (HRAI)?

High-Risk AI refers to AI systems subject to enhanced governance obligations under the EU AI Act (2024/1689).

Hochrisiko-KI bezeichnet KI-Systeme, die gemäß der EU-KI-Verordnung (2024/1689) erweiterten Governance-Verpflichtungen unterliegen.

A magas kockázatú AI az EU AI Act (2024/1689) alapján fokozott governance kötelezettségek alá eső AI rendszerekre utal.

What is AI Act Decision Support? Was ist KI-Act-Entscheidungsunterstützung? Mi az AI Act döntéstámogatás?

AI Act decision support helps executives determine which obligations apply and when escalation is necessary.

KI-Act-Entscheidungsunterstützung hilft Führungskräften dabei, festzustellen, welche Pflichten gelten und wann eine Eskalation erforderlich ist.

Az AI Act döntéstámogatás segít a döntéshozóknak meghatározni, hogy mely kötelezettségek alkalmazandók és mikor szükséges az eszkaláció.
